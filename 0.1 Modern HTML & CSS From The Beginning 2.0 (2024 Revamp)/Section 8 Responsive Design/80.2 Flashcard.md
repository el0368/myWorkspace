### **Flashcard 1**

Front:

What are the vh and vw units in CSS?

Back:

vh stands for Viewport Height and vw stands for Viewport Width. They are relative units used to size elements based on the dimensions of the browser's viewport (the visible window area).

---

### **Flashcard 2**

Front:

How are vh and vw units calculated?

Back:

They represent a percentage of the viewport's dimensions.

- `1vh` = 1% of the viewport's total height.
    
- `1vw` = 1% of the viewport's total width.
    

For example, `height: 50vh;` makes an element's height equal to 50% of the viewport's height.

---

### **Flashcard 3**

Front:

What is the key difference between height: 100%; and height: 100vh;?

Back:

height: 100%; sizes an element to be 100% of its parent container's height.

`height: 100vh;` sizes an element to be 100% of the **viewport's** height, ignoring the size of its parent.

---

### **Flashcard 4**

Front:

What is a common use case for the vh unit in web design? 🖥️

Back:

Creating a full-screen "hero" section at the top of a webpage. By setting its height to 100vh, the section will always fill the entire visible screen when the page first loads.

---

### **Flashcard 5**

Front:

How can the vw unit be used for responsive typography?

Back:

It can be used to set the font-size of headings. This makes the heading text scale up and down fluidly as the viewport's width changes.

Example: `font-size: 7vw;`

---

### **Flashcard 6**

Front:

Why is using vw for font-size generally avoided for paragraphs?

Back:

The scaling effect is often too extreme for body text. On very wide screens, paragraph text can become too large and difficult to read. This technique is better suited for large, impactful headings.
