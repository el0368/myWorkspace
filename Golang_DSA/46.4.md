### The "Room and Bulb" Analogy 💡

To make the behavior of the two previous recursive examples more intuitive, the instructor introduces an analogy involving three nested rooms, each containing a light bulb.

#### Scenario 1: Action Before the Recursive Step (Like `fun1`)

In this scenario, the instructions are:
1.  **"First step is switch on bulb."**
2.  **"Second step is go to the next room."**

He traces the execution:
* You start in Room 1, follow step one, and **switch on bulb 1**. Then you follow step two and enter Room 2.
* In Room 2, you repeat the steps: **switch on bulb 2** and enter Room 3.
* In Room 3, you **switch on bulb 3** and try to go to the next room.
* At this point, "There is no next room further," so the process terminates. You then exit the rooms—from 3 to 2, then 2 to 1—but since the work was already done, you "**simply come out. Nothing will do**" on the way back.

The result is that the bulbs are switched on in the order **`1, 2, 3`**. This directly compares to the first Go example (`fun1`), where the print operation happens *before* the recursive call.

***

#### Scenario 2: Action After the Recursive Step (Like `fun2`)

Here, the instructor swaps the order of the instructions:
1.  **"First step is go to the next room."**
2.  **"Second step is switch On bulb."**

He traces the new execution:
* You start in Room 1. The first instruction is to go to the next room, so you immediately enter Room 2 without touching the bulb.
* In Room 2, the first instruction is again to go to the next room, so you enter Room 3.
* In Room 3, you again try to go to the next room.
* At this point, "there is no next room," so that step fails. Now you perform the second step: "**switch On bulb.** So the first bulb to be switched On is **bulb 3**."
* You then return to the previous room (Room 2), where you now perform its pending second step and **switch on bulb 2**.
* Finally, you return to Room 1 and perform its pending second step, **switching on bulb 1**.

The result is that the bulbs are switched on in the order **`3, 2, 1`**. This directly compares to the second Go example (`fun2`), where the print operation happens *after* the recursive call has finished and is returning.
