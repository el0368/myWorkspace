Ok, let's talk about a very fast and powerful type of complexity. This one is a favorite among programmers.

---

### Logarithmic Time - O(log n) ⚡️

We call this one Logarithmic Time, and we write it as $O(log n)$.

This type of algorithm is extremely efficient.1 It means that even if your number of items `n` gets very, very large, the time it takes to complete the task grows very slowly.2 For example, if you have a million items in a slice, the task might take 20 steps. If you double that to two million items, the task might only take 21 steps. The time barely increases.

---

#### How this looks in the real world

The best way to understand this is to think about searching for a word in a big, physical dictionary. Let's say the dictionary has 1,000 pages (`n = 1000`).

You wouldn't start at page 1 and read every single page until you find the word. That would be `$O(n)` and would take a long time.

Instead, you would do this:

1. Open the dictionary right to the middle, at page 500.
    
2. Look at the words on that page. You can immediately tell if your word comes _before_ or _after_ page 500.
    
3. With that single action, you have just eliminated **half the dictionary**! You can completely ignore 500 pages.
    

Now you only have 500 pages left to worry about. What do you do? The same thing again! You open to the middle of that remaining section and eliminate another half. You find the word you're looking for incredibly fast.

---

#### How this looks in programming

The key idea is that with every single step, you are **cutting the problem in half**.

In Go, the most famous example of this is a **binary search**.3 This is a very common algorithm for finding an item in a **slice**, but it only works if the slice is already **sorted**. Because it's sorted, you can look at the middle item and know instantly whether to continue searching in the left half or the right half.

So, when you are looking at your Go code, if you see a `for` loop where the main variable controlling the loop is being **divided by 2** in every step (instead of just being incremented or decremented by 1), that is a very strong clue that you are looking at a super-efficient `$O(log n)` algorithm.
