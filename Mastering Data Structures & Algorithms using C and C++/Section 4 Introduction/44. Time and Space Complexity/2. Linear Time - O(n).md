### Linear Time - O(n) 📈

So, the first and most common type of time complexity is called Linear Time. We write this as $O(n)$.

This is a very simple idea. It just means that the time it takes for your program to finish grows in a straight, direct line with the number of items you are working with. If you double the number of items, the time will roughly double. If you have 10 times more items, it will take about 10 times more time.1 It scales very predictably.

Imagine you have a stack of books, and your job is to write your name on the first page of every single book.

- If you have 10 books (`n=10`), it will take you a certain amount of time.
    
- If you have 100 books (`n=100`), it will take you 10 times longer.
    

The total time depends directly on the number of books, `n`. This is a linear task.

---

#### How this looks in programming

In Go, this is very common when you are working with a **slice**. If the work you need to do requires you to look at, or "touch," every single item in that slice, then the time complexity will be `$O(n)`.

What kind of work is this? Here are some simple examples:

- **Finding the sum of all numbers** in a slice. You have to add the first number, then the second, then the third, and so on, until you reach the end.
    
- **Finding the largest number** in a slice. You have to look at every number to be sure you have found the biggest one.
    
- **Searching for a specific item** in a slice, if the slice is not sorted.2 You have to check the first item, then the second, then the third, and keep going until you find it or reach the end.
    

In all of these cases, you have to make one "single pass" over the data. You can't skip any items. In a Go program, this work is almost always done with a single `for` loop that goes from the beginning of the slice to the end.
