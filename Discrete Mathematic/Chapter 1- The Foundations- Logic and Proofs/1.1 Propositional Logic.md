## 1.1 Propositional Logic: The Foundation

Propositional logic is the branch of logic that studies propositions and the relationships between them. It serves as the fundamental building block for more complex logical reasoning in mathematics and computer science.

### Propositions

A **proposition** is the most basic element. It is a declarative sentence that is definitively either **True (T)** or **False (F)**, but not both. We typically represent propositions with lowercase letters like p,q,r,s.

- **Example of a proposition:**
    
    - p: "The sun rises in the east." (This is True)
        
    - q: "2 + 2 = 5." (This is False)
        
- **What is NOT a proposition?**
    
    - "What is your name?" (This is a question)
        
    - "Solve this problem." (This is a command)
        
    - "x>10" (This is a predicate; its truth value depends on the value of x)
        

---

### Logical Connectives

Logical connectives are operators used to combine simple propositions to form more complex statements, known as **compound propositions**.

#### Negation (NOT)

This connective reverses the truth value of a proposition.

- **Symbol:** ¬
    
- **Reads as:** "not"
    
- **Example:** If p is "It is sunny," then ¬p is "It is not sunny."
    
- Truth Table:
    
    | p | ¬p |
    
    |:---:|:---:|
    
    | T | F |
    
    | F | T |
    

#### Conjunction (AND)

A conjunction is true only when _both_ propositions it connects are true.

- **Symbol:** ∧
    
- **Reads as:** "and"
    
- **Example:** Let p be "It is sunny" and q be "It is warm." Then p∧q is "It is sunny and it is warm." This compound statement is only true if both conditions are met.
    
- Truth Table:
    
    | p | q | p∧q |
    
    |:---:|:---:|:---:|
    
    | T | T | T |
    
    | T | F | F |
    
    | F | T | F |
    
    | F | F | F |
    

#### Disjunction (OR)

A disjunction is true if _at least one_ of the propositions it connects is true. This is also known as an "inclusive OR."

- **Symbol:** ∨
    
- **Reads as:** "or"
    
- **Example:** Let p be "I will have coffee" and q be "I will have tea." Then p∨q is "I will have coffee or I will have tea." This is true if I have coffee, if I have tea, or if I have both. It is only false if I have neither.
    
- Truth Table:
    
    | p | q | p∨q |
    
    |:---:|:---:|:---:|
    
    | T | T | T |
    
    | T | F | T |
    
    | F | T | T |
    
    | F | F | F |
    

#### Conditional (IF...THEN)

Also known as an implication, this statement takes the form "if p, then q." The proposition p is called the **hypothesis** (or antecedent), and q is the **conclusion** (or consequent). The conditional statement p→q is only false when a true hypothesis leads to a false conclusion.

- **Symbol:** →
    
- **Reads as:** "if...then," "implies"
    
- **Example:** Let p be "It is raining" and q be "The ground is wet." Then p→q is "If it is raining, then the ground is wet."
    
    - If it's raining (T) and the ground is wet (T), the statement is **True**.
        
    - If it's raining (T) and the ground is dry (F), the statement is **False** (the promise was broken).
        
    - If it's not raining (F), the ground can be wet or dry. The statement is considered **True** in both cases because the initial condition wasn't met, so the "promise" was never tested. This is called being _vacuously true_.
        
- Truth Table:
    
    | p | q | p→q |
    
    |:---:|:---:|:---:|
    
    | T | T | T |
    
    | T | F | F |
    
    | F | T | T |
    
    | F | F | T |
    

#### Biconditional (IF AND ONLY IF)

This statement asserts that p and q have the same truth value. It is equivalent to (p→q)∧(q→p).

- **Symbol:** ↔
    
- **Reads as:** "if and only if," "iff"
    
- **Example:** Let p be "You can vote" and q be "You are at least 18 years old." Then p↔q is "You can vote if and only if you are at least 18 years old." The statement is true if you vote and are over 18, or if you don't vote and are under 18. It is false if one is true but the other is not.
    
- Truth Table:
    
    | p | q | p↔q |
    
    |:---:|:---:|:---:|
    
    | T | T | T |
    
    | T | F | F |
    
    | F | T | F |
    
    | F | F | T |

### Truth Tables for Compound Propositions

A **truth table** is a powerful tool used to determine the truth value of a compound proposition for every possible combination of truth values of its atomic components.

Let's construct a truth table for the compound proposition (p∨q)→¬p.

Step 1: Create columns for the atomic propositions, p and q.

Step 2: Fill in all possible combinations of T and F. For two variables, there are 22=4 rows.

Step 3: Create columns for the intermediate expressions. Here, that's (p∨q) and ¬p.

Step 4: Evaluate these intermediate columns based on the truth tables for their connectives.

Step 5: Create the final column for the entire compound proposition and evaluate it using the results from the intermediate columns.

Here is the completed table:

|p|q|p∨q|¬p|(p∨q)→¬p|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|T|T|T|F|F|
|T|F|T|F|F|
|F|T|T|T|T|
|F|F|F|T|T|

This table shows us that the statement (p∨q)→¬p is true only when p is false.
