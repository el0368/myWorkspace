# Briefing: An Operating Systems Course Overview

## Executive Summary

This document outlines the structure and philosophy of a comprehensive course on operating systems, designed with a strong emphasis on the perspective of a back-end engineer. The course is built upon a foundational principle of inquisitiveness, consistently encouraging learners to ask "Why?" a particular technology exists to understand the core problems it was designed to solve. A recurring theme is the concept of "cost," evaluating every operation—from a function call to memory access—in terms of its performance and resource implications. The curriculum provides a deep dive into the core components of an operating system, including processes, memory management, the CPU, storage, and networking sockets. Learners are urged to maintain a healthy skepticism towards established concepts, recognizing that all technologies are human creations subject to critique and improvement. The ultimate goal is not merely to complete the course but to fully engage with and enjoy the process of learning complex technical concepts.

## Core Philosophy and Approach

The course is guided by several key philosophical tenets that shape its content and delivery.

### The Primacy of "Why"

The instructor advocates for a first-principles approach to learning, centered on the question, "Why does it exist?" This method aims to uncover the original problem that a technology was created to solve.

"I often find that when I ask why I get to the bottom reason of the thing existing, the problem if you will, and from that problem, I understand that, oh, we built this to solve this."

This approach is critical because technology and environments evolve. A solution developed 30 years ago might be addressed differently today. The screensaver is used as a prime example: it was invented to prevent screen burn-in on older monitors, a problem that no longer exists with modern displays. Yet, the feature persisted long after its core purpose became obsolete because people "forgot the reason." By consistently asking why, learners can avoid maintaining outdated solutions and potentially devise better ones.

### The Concept of "Cost"

A central analytical lens for the course is the "cost" associated with every computational action. This is especially relevant for back-end engineers concerned with performance and efficiency.

"You will see me go. Always back to this thing cost. How much does it cost now? As as a back end engineer. You know, anything that I do, I always ask how much it cost, how much it cost, how much cost?"

This analysis applies to all levels of the system, from making a function call to CPU-memory interaction. Understanding these costs provides developers with the confidence and caution needed to build high-performance applications.

### A Call for Healthy Skepticism

Learners are explicitly encouraged to question all presented material and established conventions, including the necessity of operating systems themselves.

"Everything I say and everything was presented here have a healthy criticism because nothing is written on stone. These are people who wrote all of this stuff and don't feel that this is the only way of doing things. I want you to be skeptical of everything."

This mindset fosters critical thinking and acknowledges that technology is not immutable but a product of its time, open to re-evaluation and innovation.

## Detailed Course Outline

The course is structured into several thematic sections, moving from foundational concepts to advanced, specialized topics.

### Section 1: The Rationale for an Operating System

This introductory section establishes the course's core philosophy by examining the fundamental question: "Why do we even need an operating system?" It sets the stage for a critical exploration of the problems that operating systems were designed to address.

### Section 2: The Anatomy of a Process

This section introduces the process, described as the "main driver" of the operating system.

- **Definition:** A process is defined as a "program in motion"—a live, running instance of a static program file (e.g., `Chrome.exe`).
- **Analogy:** An analogy is drawn from object-oriented programming: a program is like a **class**, and a process is like an **object** or instance of that class. Multiple processes can be created from a single program.
- **Characteristics:** Each process has its own "personality" and can exist in various states, such as sleeping, active, or resource-hungry ("very, very hungry"). Processes can also leak resources.

### Section 3: Memory Management

This module explores memory, the environment where processes live.

- **Nature of Memory:** Described as an "ephemeral thing" that stores bytes and is lost when power is turned off, but prized for its speed.
- **Topics Covered:** The section will detail concepts such as RAM, DRAM, Direct Memory Access (DMA), and the powerful concept of virtual memory.
- **Focus on Cost:** The cost of accessing memory and the mechanics of operations like function calls will be scrutinized to provide a deeper appreciation for their performance impact.

### Section 4: The Central Processing Unit (CPU)

This section delves into the "heart of the execution," the CPU.

- **Function:** The CPU is responsible for executing the instructions of processes, using memory as a "scratchpad."
- **Architectural Styles:** The course will explore different CPU architectures, such as complex instruction set computing (CISC), like Intel's approach, versus reduced instruction set computing (RISC), which favors multiple simple instructions over one complex one.
- **Key Components:** In-depth coverage will be given to caches (L1, L2, L3), the Translation Lookaside Buffer (TLB), instruction lifecycles, and parallelism. It is noted that developers of high-performance applications, such as gaming engines and databases, must have a deep understanding of CPU workings.

### Section 5: Advanced Process Management

Building on the initial "anatomy" section, this module covers intermediate to advanced topics in process and thread management.

- **Key Concepts:**
    - File Descriptors
    - Threads vs. Processes
    - Context Switching
    - Kernel Mode vs. User Mode (Mode Switch)
    - Concurrency vs. Parallelism
    - Multithreading vs. Multiprocessing

### Section 6: Storage Management

This section focuses on persistent storage, a critical area for back-end engineers and databases.

- **File Systems:** Presented as an OS-level abstraction that simplifies storage for applications by organizing it into files and directories.
- **Critique and Value:** While file systems are valuable for abstracting diverse hardware (NVMe, SATA, Zoned Namespaces SSDs), the course offers a critical view, suggesting that building databases on this "very complex system" may be a fundamentally flawed approach.
- **I/O Operations:** The module will explore what happens during an I/O operation, noting that "IO" is one of the most overloaded terms in operating systems. The distinction between the terms "page" and "block" will also be clarified.

### Section 7: Socket Management and Networking

Described as a "favorite thing" of the instructor, this section is essential for back-end engineers who deal with network requests.

- **Networking Fundamentals:** Covers TCP, UDP, and the role of protocols.
- **The Kernel's Role:** Discusses how the TCP/IP stack is built directly into the kernel. This design was historically beneficial but is increasingly seen as a bottleneck due to the significant memory copying required between kernel space and user space.
- **Abstractions and Their Consequences:** The POSIX API (e.g., `read`, `write`) provides ease of use but hides immense complexity. A simple `read` call can block, which led to the invention of **asynchronous I/O**.

### Section 8: Miscellaneous Advanced Concepts

This final, flexible section serves as a "catch-all" for important topics that do not fit neatly into the other categories.

- **Rationale:** The instructor compares the real world to "squiggly" lines like mountains and rivers, rather than rigid squares, justifying the need for a section that isn't strictly siloed.
- **Included Topics:**
    - Copy-on-Write
    - Virtualization
    - Containers
    - Kernel Switch
    - Compilers and Linkers
- **Expandability:** This section is designed to grow over time with new lectures and topics suggested by learners.

## Concluding Philosophy on Learning

The course concludes not with technical content, but with advice on the mindset for learning. The emphasis is on valuing the journey over the destination.

"The joy is not to reach the end and say, hey, I have a certificate. The joy is ... to enjoy learning."

Learners are encouraged to "lose yourself" in the activity of the moment—be it writing an app, debugging, or performance monitoring—because that is where the real joy is found. Thinking about the finish line can lead to disappointment. Using the analogy of learning Japanese kanji, the instructor notes the beauty in each character, rather than worrying about the 2,000 that must be learned. The course itself is presented as a long journey to be taken slowly and deliberately.
