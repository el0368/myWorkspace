## The Absolute Basics: What Are We Even Doing?

Before we write any code, let's understand the two big ideas: **Data Structures** and **Algorithms**. Think of it like cooking.

### 1. What is a Data Structure? 🧺

A **data structure** is simply a way to **store and organize data**.1 That's it. It's the "pantry" or "refrigerator" for your information.

Imagine your grocery list. You could write it as a simple, single list:

- Milk
    
- Eggs
    
- Cheese
    
- Bread
    
- Bagels
    

This is a simple data structure called a **list** (or an **array**).2

Now, what if you organized it by where things are in the store?

- **DAIRY:**
    
    - Milk3
        
    - Eggs
        
    - Cheese
        
- **BAKERY:**
    
    - Bread
        
    - Bagels
        

This is a _different_ data structure. The data is the same, but its organization is different. The way you organize your data can make it much easier or harder to use.4

**In programming, a data structure is a container for data, with rules about how to add, remove, and access that data.5**

---

### 2. What is an Algorithm? 🧑‍🍳

An **algorithm** is a **step-by-step recipe** for getting something done.6 It's the instructions you follow to work with your data.

Using our grocery list analogy, an "algorithm" could be your plan for shopping:

1. Start at the entrance of the store.
    
2. Go to the "DAIRY" section.
    
3. Put all items from your DAIRY list into the cart.
    
4. Go to the "BAKERY" section.
    
5. Put all items from your BAKERY list into the cart.
    
6. Go to the checkout.
    

This is an algorithm. A different algorithm would be to wander around the store randomly until you find everything you need. One recipe is clearly better than the other!

**In programming, an algorithm is a set of instructions to perform a task, like finding an item, sorting a list, or saving information.7**

---

## Why They Matter Together

The key idea is this:

> The choice of **data structure** has a huge impact on how efficient your **algorithm** can be.

Using the organized (by aisle) grocery list makes your shopping algorithm much faster.

Our goal is to learn about common data structures (like arrays, stacks, and queues) and common algorithms (like searching and sorting) so we can write smart, efficient programs.
